When new evidence should change an agent’s decision

Withdrawing a conclusion is easy. Knowing which parts of the reasoning still hold is harder.

Raghav Maini · September 12, 2026 · Revised October 1, 2026

When Knight Capital’s trading system malfunctioned in August 2012, one part of the system recognized that customer orders had already been filled. The part sending orders to the market did not receive that information and kept trading. A deployment error had activated obsolete code on one server. The SEC’s account makes an unusually precise distinction: the organization possessed the relevant fact, but the component acting on its behalf continued as though it did not.

This was conventional software, not a language model. It exposes a problem that a more articulate agent does not automatically solve: where, exactly, does new knowledge acquire the power to stop an old plan?

I approached that question while building a personal tool to investigate salvage-car rebuilds. I haven’t bought a car at auction. I wanted to understand what I could justify bidding before becoming attached to a project. The application ties repair reviews to a recorded scope; changing that scope reopens the reviews. It is deliberately conservative. It also treats a difficult question—what did this change invalidate?—as though every change had the same answer.

Consider a repair quote covering visible body damage, followed by an inspection that identifies structural damage. This is a hypothetical case, but the distinction matters: the quote has not become fraudulent, and its price for the original work may still stand. What loses support is the inference that it covers the rebuild. A bid ceiling derived from that inference needs to be reopened. A separately verified gearbox specification need not be.

Jon Doyle’s work on truth maintenance supplies a more exact vocabulary than “remember the context.” His 1977 system recorded justifications for beliefs so that changing an assumption could change the conclusions that depended on it. A conclusion could also have another justification that remained valid. In his 1983 formulation, Doyle stressed that acceptability was relative to the recorded justifications. The mechanism did not guarantee logical consistency.

Applied to the quote, that means retaining the source while withdrawing the conclusion it no longer supports. It also means distinguishing “unsupported” from “false.” New damage does not establish that the car is uneconomic; it establishes that the previous calculation no longer settles the question. Confusing those two would turn sensible caution into automatic rejection.

There is a cost to making this selective. Someone has to specify the dependency between the quote’s coverage and the bid calculation. If that relationship is missing, a beautifully maintained record can preserve precisely the conclusion that ought to change. Invalidating the whole scope tolerates more unnecessary work in exchange for depending on fewer judgments about relevance.

Rich Sutton’s “The Bitter Lesson” challenges the instinct to solve every such problem by encoding more human understanding. His historical argument is that general search and learning have repeatedly outgrown specialized designs built around researchers’ knowledge. That does not refute Doyle’s bookkeeping. It does challenge treating a hand-built map of dependencies as the final account of what can matter.

For a short, inexpensive task, asking the model to reconsider all the evidence may be simpler than maintaining that map. Explicit dependencies become more attractive when investigations are costly or several decisions reuse the same work. They then introduce another possible error: a mistaken account of which conclusions are independent. Learning could help discover those relationships; mechanically following them cannot establish that they are complete.

Even a correct revision has to reach the ongoing plan. In his account of building agents, Armin Ronacher describes injecting background state changes into the agent loop. When a recovery attempt proceeds from broken data, his system tells the agent that it may need to back up and redo an earlier step. This practitioner observation adds something the dependency graph alone lacks: correcting stored state and redirecting work are separate operations.

In the repair case, merely appending the inspection leaves the agent to rediscover its consequence. An explicit retraction can tell it which estimate is no longer supported and why. But that message can itself become stale before an action is taken. H. T. Kung and John Robinson’s 1981 work on optimistic concurrency control separates reading and computation from validation before changes become visible. It offers a useful boundary here: interpreting the evidence and checking that the recorded state has not changed are different jobs. A revision check cannot decide whether a quote covers structural damage; an interpretation cannot prevent a concurrent update.

The consequence is that retaining more work requires making stronger claims about it. A coarse reset says little about relevance and sacrifices usable research. Selective revision saves that research by asserting that some conclusions survive the change. The more decisions reuse an estimate, the more consequential that assertion becomes: an omitted dependency can let one obsolete premise support several apparently current decisions.

This changes what I want from an agent’s explanation. A persuasive account of why a recommendation made sense yesterday is insufficient. I want to know which parts still stand after today’s evidence, and what supports leaving them alone. That is a more demanding purpose for an explanation than making the answer sound reasonable. It has to help determine how much of the answer can survive being challenged.
